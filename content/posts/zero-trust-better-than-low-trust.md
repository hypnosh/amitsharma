title: "Zero Trust Gives a Better Experience Than Low Trust"
---

This morning I had to leave home while our new cleaning staff was still working.

I could have stayed around and kept an eye on things. After all, I don't know them well yet.

Instead, I locked the bedroom, told them about it, and left.

They had access to everything they needed to do their job. I had protected the one space they didn't need access to. When they finished, they would close the main door behind them.

No supervision. No awkwardness. No need to make a judgement about whether they were trustworthy.

That got me thinking about the difference between **low trust and zero trust**.

Low trust says: *I don't trust you, so I need to watch you.*

Zero trust says: *I don't need to trust you, because I've designed the system so that you don't need access to what you don't need.*

That's a much better experience.

The distinction matters because low-trust systems tend to push the burden onto people.

We see this everywhere in products.

You don't trust the user, so you make them enter another OTP.

You don't trust the transaction, so you make them confirm it three times.

You don't trust the employee, so you add another approval layer.

You don't trust the customer to understand what they're doing, so you add warnings and confirmation dialogs everywhere.

Sometimes these controls are necessary. But often, we're using human vigilance to compensate for poor system design.

Zero trust takes a different approach.

Assume that every request might be risky. Give each person or process only the access it actually needs. Verify what matters. Contain the consequences when something goes wrong.

And, crucially, **make the system carry the burden of that uncertainty, not the user.**

That's why zero trust can actually produce a more trusting experience.

A well-designed system can be extremely sceptical underneath while feeling remarkably effortless on the surface.

Your bank doesn't need to ask you whether you are really you every thirty seconds if it can continuously assess the risk of what you're doing.

A workplace doesn't need to rely entirely on whether an employee is trustworthy if permissions are designed around what that employee actually needs to access.

A product doesn't need to prevent every possible mistake with a warning if it can make important actions reversible.

The principle is broader than security:

> **Don't solve a trust problem with surveillance when you can solve it with boundaries.**

There is an important difference between saying *"I don't trust you"* and saying *"you don't need access to that."*

The first creates tension.

The second creates clarity.

And perhaps that's one of the underrated jobs of product design: not figuring out whom we can trust, but designing systems where **trust becomes less necessary**.

The best systems don't require us to constantly watch people.

They make it safe enough to look away.
